| Practical Winding Guides

Short, field-tested notes for rewinding workshops and maintenance electricians.

Winding Design

Stator Winding Design Basics

Count the slots, poles and coils before you strip anything, and record the original turns, wire diameter and connection (star or delta) for every phase. The turns per coil set the magnetic flux: too few turns and the motor draws excess current and overheats; too many and it loses torque. Keep the slot fill factor roughly between 35% and 55% so the coils fit without damaging the enamel.

Single-Phase Motors

Single-Phase Capacitor Wiring

A single-phase motor needs a start winding and a capacitor to create the rotating field. Identify the main and auxiliary windings with a multimeter first — the auxiliary winding usually has the higher resistance. Wire the run capacitor permanently in series with the auxiliary winding, and the start capacitor (if fitted) through the centrifugal switch so it drops out once the motor reaches about 75% of full speed.

Starting Methods

Star-Delta (Y-Δ) Starting

For motors of about 11 kW and above on a weak supply, star-delta starting cuts the starting current to roughly one third of direct-on-line. The motor starts in star, then a timer switches it to delta after a few seconds. Never use star-delta on a motor nameplated for delta-only at your supply voltage, and set the changeover timer so the motor is close to full speed before switching.

Testing & Diagnostics

Insulation Testing (Megger Test)

Before energising a rewound motor, test the insulation resistance between windings and frame with a 500 V megger for low-voltage motors (1,000 V for larger machines). A healthy dry winding reads well above 100 MΩ at room temperature; anything under 1–2 MΩ needs drying or investigation. Always discharge the winding after the test and record the reading with the winding temperature.

Drives & Inverters

VFDs and Insulation Stress

A variable frequency drive switches the voltage thousands of times per second, and the fast voltage rise can stress the insulation of the first turns of each coil — especially on older motors and long cable runs. Use inverter-rated enamel wire, keep motor cables short, and consider a dv/dt filter or output reactor when the cable exceeds 20–30 metres. At low speeds the shaft fan moves less air, so watch the temperature.

Maintenance

Bearing Care and Greasing

More than half of all motor failures start at the bearings. Listen for a rough or growling sound, check for excess vibration, and feel the bearing housing temperature during a run. Grease sparingly and only with the specified type — over-greasing overheats the bearing just as surely as running it dry. Replace bearings in pairs, and never hammer a new bearing onto the shaft through its outer race.

| Frequently Asked Questions

Quick answers to the questions winding shops ask most often.

Turns set the magnetic flux. Fewer turns than the original design raises the flux density, so the motor draws more no-load current and runs hot. More turns lowers the flux and the torque. Small changes (±5%) are usually tolerated, but a rewind should match the original turns unless you are deliberately redesigning the motor for a different voltage.

Aluminium conducts only about 61% as well as copper, so you need roughly 1.28 times the cross-section (about 1.13 times the diameter) for the same resistance, which is hard to fit in the same slots. Copper remains the standard choice for rewinds where slot space is tight.
As a rule of thumb, from about 11 kW (15 HP) upwards, or whenever direct starting would dip the supply voltage enough to disturb other equipment. The exact point depends on your transformer size and supply impedance — check the voltage drop at start with the calculator above before deciding.
A common starting point is about 70 µF per kW at 230 V / 50 Hz, then fine-tune by measuring the auxiliary winding current — the correct capacitor makes the main and auxiliary winding currents roughly equal under load. Use the capacitor calculator above for the estimate, and always use a capacitor rated for continuous AC motor duty at the right voltage.
Motor torque falls with the square of the voltage, so a 10% voltage drop costs about 19% of starting torque — enough to stall a loaded pump or compressor at start. Long cable runs also waste energy as heat. Keeping the drop within 5% protects starting performance and efficiency.
