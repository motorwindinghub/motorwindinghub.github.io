VFDs and Insulation Stress

A variable frequency drive is one of the best things you can fit to a motor — and one of the hardest on its insulation. The drive does not feed the motor a smooth sine wave. It feeds it thousands of sharp voltage pulses every second, and those pulses attack the enamel on the first turns of each coil. Understanding why this happens is the difference between a motor that runs for years on a drive and one that fails in months.

What a VFD actually sends to the motor

A modern drive builds its output from a DC bus of roughly 560 V (on a 400 V supply) by switching its output transistors on and off thousands of times per second — pulse width modulation, or PWM. The motor's inductance smooths those pulses into an average current that looks close to sinusoidal, but the voltage at the motor terminals is still a train of steep-edged pulses. The rise time of each pulse can be under 0.1 microseconds, which means the voltage changes at a rate of several thousand volts per microsecond. Ordinary mains voltage rises gently by comparison, and ordinary enamel wire was never designed for that.

The reflected wave: why the peaks double

The real damage comes from the cable between the drive and the motor. A fast pulse travels down the cable as a wave, hits the motor — which looks like a high impedance to a fast pulse — and reflects back. The reflected wave adds to the incoming wave, and the voltage at the motor terminals can briefly reach nearly twice the DC bus voltage: over 1,000 V peak on a 400 V system. This is called the reflected-wave or voltage-doubling effect, and it is worst when the cable is long enough for the pulse to develop — roughly 10–15 metres and above — and when the drive's rise time is very fast. The first turns of each coil take the worst of it, because the voltage distributes unevenly across the winding during the first instants of each pulse.

Partial discharge and the first turns

When the peak voltage between adjacent turns exceeds the breakdown strength of the tiny air gaps in the winding, microscopic sparks — partial discharges — occur inside the insulation. Each spark is tiny, but there are thousands of them every second, and they slowly erode the enamel. Standard enamel wire resists this for a while, but on an older motor, or one with a long cable, the first turn of the first coil can fail within months. This is why VFD failures so often show up as a turn-to-turn short in the first coil, rather than a general insulation failure.

A dv/dt filter (a small reactor and capacitor network at the drive output) slows the pulse edges and cuts the peak voltage. An output reactor does part of the same job more cheaply. A sine-wave filter reconstructs a near-sinusoidal voltage and is the safest option for very long cables, though it costs more and needs to be sized to the drive. For a rewind that will run on a drive, always use inverter-duty (corona-resistant) enamel wire with a thicker, tougher insulation build — it costs a little more and is much harder to damage during winding, but it is the correct material for the job.

Carrier frequency: a trade-off

The drive's carrier (switching) frequency is usually adjustable between about 2 kHz and 16 kHz. A higher carrier frequency makes the motor run quieter and smoother, but it increases the number of pulses per second — and therefore the number of partial-discharge events — and it increases the drive's own losses. A lower carrier frequency is kinder to the insulation and the drive, at the cost of audible noise. For most industrial motors a setting of 2–4 kHz is a sensible compromise, unless the application specifically needs quiet running.

Low-speed cooling

A second, separate VFD problem has nothing to do with insulation: cooling. A standard TEFC motor cools itself with a fan on its own shaft, so at 20 Hz the fan moves less than half the air it moves at 50 Hz, while the motor may still be producing nearly full torque and full losses. Running a standard motor continuously below about 25–30 Hz at full load will overheat it. The fixes are to derate the motor, fit a separately powered cooling fan, or use a motor rated for inverter duty with a constant-torque speed range. Watch the temperature on the first few runs — a winding temperature check tells you more than any calculation.

Rewinding a motor for VFD duty

  • Use inverter-duty, corona-resistant enamel wire (thicker build, e.g. grade 2).
  • Reinforce the first coil of each phase — extra slot liner and phase separator paper.
  • Use phase insulation between coil groups, not just slot liners.
  • Keep the varnish impregnation thorough — voids are where partial discharge starts.
  • Record on the nameplate or in the job file that the motor is VFD-rated, with the maximum cable length.

VFD installation checklist

  1. Check the motor. Confirm the motor is inverter-duty rated, or that the cable run is short and the carrier frequency will be kept low. Old standard motors on long cables are the worst combination.
  2. Use the right cable. Fit symmetrical three-core plus earth cable, or shielded motor cable, with the shield earthed at the drive end. Keep the cable as short as the layout allows.
  3. Fit the filter. On cable runs over 30 m, fit a dv/dt filter or output reactor at the drive; over 100 m, use a sine-wave filter.
  4. Set the carrier frequency. Start low (2–4 kHz) on older motors and raise it only if the acoustic noise is a problem and the motor insulation allows it.
  5. Set the motor data. Enter the nameplate voltage, current, speed and power into the drive and run the auto-tune if the drive offers one.
  6. Check the first turns. After the first week of running, megger-test the motor and compare with the reading taken before the drive was fitted.
  7. Watch low-speed running. If the motor will run below 20 Hz for long periods, check the temperature rise — the shaft fan moves little air at low speed, and a separate cooling fan may be needed.
  8. Earth properly. Bond the motor frame, the drive and the cable shield to a common earth point to keep bearing currents and interference down.

Frequently asked questions

Can any standard motor run on a VFD?

Yes, with care. A standard motor on a short cable with a low carrier frequency will usually run for years. The risk grows with cable length, carrier frequency and the age of the insulation. Motors made before the 1990s, or rewound with standard enamel wire, are the most vulnerable — on those, keep the cable short or fit a filter.

Why does the motor get hotter on a VFD at low speed?

Two reasons. First, the shaft-mounted fan turns with the motor, so at 20 Hz it moves less than half the air it moves at 50 Hz. Second, the drive output is not a pure sine wave, so the winding carries extra harmonic currents that add heat. Together they can push a motor that was fine on mains supply into overheating on a drive.

Do I need a sine-wave filter or is a dv/dt filter enough?

For most installations up to about 100 m, a dv/dt filter or output reactor is enough — it slows the voltage edges and keeps the peak voltage within safe limits. Above 100 m, or where the motor is old and the insulation is unknown, a sine-wave filter is the safer choice because it restores a near-sine waveform at the motor terminals.

Safety note: A drive's DC bus stays charged for minutes after the power is switched off — always wait for the drive's display to go fully dark and check the bus voltage with a meter before opening the drive or working on the motor cable. Never megger-test a motor while it is connected to a drive.
