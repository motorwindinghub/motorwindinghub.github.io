Star-Delta (Y-Δ) Starting

A large motor switched straight onto the supply can draw six or seven times its rated current for the first second. On a weak supply that dip can dim the lights, drop out other contactors, and annoy the whole factory. Star-delta starting is the oldest and cheapest fix: start the motor in star, let it run up, then switch it to delta for normal running.

Why starting current is a problem

At standstill a motor looks almost like a short circuit to the supply — the rotor is not turning, so there is no back EMF to limit the current. A direct-on-line (DOL) start therefore pulls roughly 6–7 times rated current. For a small motor the supply barely notices. For a 15 kW motor drawing 170 A at start on a 100 kVA transformer, the voltage dip can be 15% or more, and every other machine on the same board feels it. The dip also reduces the starting torque of every other motor on the supply, since torque falls with the square of the voltage.

How star-delta works

In star, each winding sees the phase voltage — 230 V on a 400 V supply — instead of the full 400 V line voltage. The voltage across each winding is 1/√3 (about 58%) of the delta value, so the current drawn from the supply falls to one third of the direct-on-line value. The price is torque: starting torque also falls to one third, so the motor accelerates more slowly and cannot start a heavy load.

Once the motor is close to full speed, a timer switches the winding connection from star to delta. At that point the motor takes the full line voltage across each winding and runs exactly as it would on a direct-on-line start, but the heavy current transient is over. The changeover itself causes a brief current spike as the motor is momentarily disconnected and reconnected, so the timer must be set so the motor is nearly at full speed before switching — if it is still crawling, the spike is almost as bad as a direct start.

The three contactors and the timer

A star-delta starter uses three contactors: the main contactor (MCM), the delta contactor (MCD) and the star contactor (MCY), plus a timer and an overload relay. The main contactor carries the full line current. The delta and star contactors each carry only the phase current, which is 58% of the line current, so they are one size smaller than the main contactor. The overload relay is normally wired in the delta circuit, where it sees phase current, and it must be set to 0.58 × the rated line current — a detail that is often set wrong.

The timer is usually set between 5 and 15 seconds, depending on the load: a fan with high inertia needs longer than a pump. The right setting is the shortest time that lets the motor reach near full speed in star. You can check it with a clamp meter: the current should have fallen close to the running value before changeover.

Cutting the starting current from 171 A to 57 A is what saves the supply — but note the torque also drops to a third. A pump or fan with a light starting load is ideal; a loaded conveyor or crusher is not.

When to use it — and when not to

Use star-delta from about 11 kW (15 HP) upwards, or whenever a direct start would dip the supply voltage enough to disturb other equipment. It is simple, cheap and well understood, and it needs no electronics.

Do not use it on a motor whose nameplate says delta-only at your supply voltage — actually a motor nameplated 400 V delta is exactly what star-delta needs (its windings are rated for 400 V in delta, and they see 230 V in star). The real trap is the reverse: a motor nameplated 230 V delta / 400 V star must never be run in delta on a 400 V supply, star-delta or not. Also avoid star-delta where the load needs high starting torque, or where the motor must start and stop frequently — the changeover transient and the contactor wear add up. For those jobs a soft starter or a variable frequency drive is the better choice.

Common faults

  • Motor stalls in star and never changes over: timer set too short, or the load torque is higher than the star torque.
  • Loud bang at changeover: timer set too long — the motor is already at speed and the reconnection transient is harsh. Shorten the time.
  • Overload trips at normal load: relay set to line current instead of 0.58 × line current, or wired in the line instead of the delta circuit.
  • Star contactor welded: changeover too slow, or the timer failed and both star and delta were briefly closed together.

Setting the changeover timer

The timer is the part most often set wrong. It should switch from star to delta when the motor is close to full speed — typically 5 to 10 seconds for a fan or pump, longer for a high-inertia load such as a crusher or a large flywheel. If it switches too early, the motor has not built up speed and the changeover produces a second current surge almost as large as a direct start. If it switches too late, the motor sits in star at reduced torque and may stall under load. The practical way to set it is to watch the current with a clamp meter during a start and set the timer a second or two after the current has fallen to near its running value.

Step-by-step commissioning

  1. Check the nameplate. Confirm the motor is rated 400 V in delta (400/690 V) and that all six winding ends are brought out to the terminal box.
  2. Verify the connections. With the supply isolated, check the winding resistances: all three phases should match, and the star point must not be linked in the terminal box.
  3. Wire the contactors. Main contactor feeds U1, V1, W1; the delta contactor joins U2–V1, V2–W1, W2–U1; the star contactor shorts U2, V2, W2 together.
  4. Set the overload. The overload relay sits in the main contactor line and sees only the phase current, so set it to 0.58 × rated current (16.5 A on a 28.5 A motor).
  5. Set the timer. Start with 8 seconds and adjust after watching the current, as described above.
  6. Test without load. Start the motor unloaded and confirm it changes over smoothly, with no bang and no second surge.
  7. Test under load. Start the driven machine and watch the current at changeover; if the motor dips in speed at the switch, lengthen the timer slightly.
  8. Check the run. After a full start, measure the running current and the temperature of the contactors after 30 minutes.

Frequently asked questions

Can any 400 V motor use star-delta starting?

No. The motor must be rated 400 V in delta, which the nameplate shows as 400/690 V, and all six winding ends must be available in the terminal box. A 230/400 V motor is already delta at 400 V with only three terminals — it cannot be star-delta started on a 400 V supply.

Why does the motor lose speed at changeover?

At the moment of switching there is a brief break in the supply, and in star the motor only makes a third of its torque. If the timer switches before the motor is near full speed, or if the load is heavy, the motor slows during the break and the delta reconnection draws a large surge. Lengthening the timer usually fixes it; if it does not, the load may need a soft starter or a drive instead.

Is the overload setting really 0.58 times rated current?

Yes, when the overload is wired in the main contactor line — which is the usual arrangement. It sees the phase current, not the line current, and in delta the phase current is the line current divided by √3 (0.58). If the overload is wired in the supply line ahead of the starter, set it to the full rated current instead.

Safety note: Star-delta starters have live terminals in the starter box even when the motor is stopped, and the changeover involves two contactors that must never close together — always fit the mechanical and electrical interlocks, test them, and isolate and lock out the supply before working inside the starter.
