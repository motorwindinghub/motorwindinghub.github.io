Guides / Testing & Diagnostics

Insulation Testing (Megger Test)

The megger test is the single most useful check you can do on a motor before energising it. It takes five minutes, needs no disassembly, and it will find damp windings, cracked insulation and earth faults that a multimeter cannot see. Every rewound motor should be tested before it leaves the shop — and every suspect motor should be tested before it goes back into service.

What insulation resistance actually measures

Insulation resistance is the resistance between the copper winding and the earthed motor frame, measured with a high DC test voltage. Good insulation is an almost perfect insulator, so the reading is in hundreds of megohms. Damp, dirty or heat-damaged insulation lets a small current leak through, and the reading falls. A multimeter on its resistance range uses only a few volts and cannot stress the insulation at all, which is why it reads "open circuit" on windings that are in fact dangerously damp. The megger applies 500 V or 1,000 V and measures the resulting leakage current, which is a real test of the insulation's condition.

Choosing the test voltage

For low-voltage motors up to 1 kV rated voltage, the standard choice is a 500 V test. For larger machines and motors rated above 1 kV, use 1,000 V. Never test electronic equipment, variable frequency drives, or surge protection devices that are still connected to the motor — disconnect them first, because the test voltage will damage them. Check the motor's documentation if you are unsure; the manufacturer usually states the test voltage and the minimum acceptable reading.

Step-by-step test

  1. Isolate the motor from the supply, lock it out, and prove it dead.
  2. Disconnect the motor leads from the starter, drive and any capacitors.
  3. Clean the terminal box — surface dirt and moisture give false low readings.
  4. Connect the megger's earth lead to the motor frame and the line lead to one phase winding.
  5. Short the other two phases together and connect them to the frame for a phase-to-earth test, or test each phase in turn.
  6. Crank or press test for 60 seconds and record the reading at 1 minute, with the winding temperature.
  7. Discharge the winding through the megger's discharge function before touching anything.

Test each phase separately, and also test between phases (winding to winding) on a motor whose phases are not already joined. A phase-to-phase fault will not show on a phase-to-earth test, and it is exactly the fault a rewind can introduce if two coils touch.

Temperature correction

Insulation resistance roughly halves for every 10 °C rise in winding temperature, so a reading taken on a hot motor always looks worse than the same winding cold. Always record the temperature with the reading, and correct back to a common reference — 40 °C is the usual one — before comparing readings taken weeks apart. A practical shortcut used in the field is to double the reading for every 10 °C below 40 °C, and halve it for every 10 °C above. Without the temperature, a reading is only half a measurement.

Reading the results

Reading at 40 °C (1 min)VerdictAction
Above 100 MΩGoodFit for service
10 – 100 MΩFairServiceable, but trend it — test again in 3–6 months
2 – 10 MΩPoorInvestigate: damp, dirt or ageing insulation
Below 2 MΩBadDry out or rewind before energising
Near 0 MΩEarth faultDo not energise — locate and repair the fault

These are field guides, not pass/fail limits carved in stone. A new rewound motor should read well above 100 MΩ; an old motor in a damp plant may live happily at 20 MΩ for years. What matters most is the trend: a reading that halves every few months is a winding on its way out, whatever the absolute number.

Polarisation index (PI)

For larger motors, the polarisation index adds a second dimension. It is the ratio of the 10-minute reading to the 1-minute reading: PI = R10 ÷ R1. Clean, dry insulation polarises slowly, so the resistance keeps rising and the PI is above 2. Damp or contaminated insulation polarises quickly and the PI drops towards 1. A PI below 1.5 on a large machine is a warning worth investigating, even if the 1-minute reading looks acceptable.

Drying a damp winding

A motor that reads low because of damp can often be saved without a rewind. The usual methods are gentle oven drying at 80–100 °C for several hours, or passing a low DC current through the windings to warm them from the inside — never more than about a third of rated current, with the rotor locked. Re-test every hour or two and stop when the reading has risen above 100 MΩ and is still climbing. If the reading plateaus low, the insulation is damaged, not just damp, and drying will not fix it.

Test voltage and minimum values

Using the wrong test voltage is the most common testing mistake. Too low a voltage will not stress the insulation enough to reveal a weakness; too high a voltage can damage a healthy winding. The table below follows the usual field practice for low-voltage motors on a 400/230 V system.

These are practical minimums for a motor going back into service, not the much higher values a new winding should show. A freshly rewound motor should read well above 100 MΩ; if it does not, find out why before the motor leaves the shop.

Common causes of a low reading

A low reading does not always mean the winding is ruined, and knowing the cause decides whether the motor can be saved. Damp is the most common: a motor stored in a humid place, or one that has been washed down, can read a few megohms and recover fully after drying. Dirt and carbon dust are next — conductive dust on the end windings gives a low reading that often clears with a careful clean and a blow-out with dry air. Ageing insulation reads low and keeps falling; unlike damp, it does not recover with drying, and the trend over months is steadily downward. A reading near zero usually means a real fault: a winding touching the frame, a damaged slot liner, or a lead chafed through where it leaves the stator. In that case drying and cleaning will not help — the fault has to be found and repaired.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a motor be megger-tested?

Critical motors deserve a test every six to twelve months, and any motor that has been idle for months should be tested before it is started again. Motors in damp or dusty places need testing more often, because their readings fall faster. Keep a log of the readings and the winding temperature each time — the trend matters more than any single number.

Can megger testing damage a good winding?

At the correct test voltage, no. A 500 V test on a 400 V motor is well within what sound insulation can withstand. Damage happens when the wrong voltage is used — for example 1,000 V on a small 230 V motor — or when the motor is tested while still connected to electronic equipment such as a drive or a soft starter, which the test voltage can destroy.

Why correct the reading to 40 °C?

Insulation resistance halves roughly every 10 °C of temperature rise, so a reading taken on a hot motor looks far worse than the same winding cold. Correcting to a standard 40 °C lets you compare readings taken months apart on equal terms, which is the only way to see a genuine downward trend.

Safety note: A megger puts 500–1,000 V DC on the winding, and the winding holds that charge after the test. Always use the instrument's discharge function and wait for the voltage to fall to zero before touching the terminals. Never megger-test a motor that is connected to the supply, a drive, or any electronic equipment.
