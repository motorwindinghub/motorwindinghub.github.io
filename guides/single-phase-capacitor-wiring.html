Single-Phase Capacitor Wiring

A single-phase supply has no rotating magnetic field, so a single-phase motor cannot start on its own. The capacitor and the auxiliary winding exist for one job only: to create a second, phase-shifted field that drags the rotor into rotation. Get the capacitor and the wiring right and the motor starts cleanly; get them wrong and it hums, overheats, or refuses to turn at all.

Why a capacitor is needed at all

A single-phase winding fed with alternating current produces a field that pulses back and forth along one axis. It has no sense of direction, so the rotor has no reason to turn either way. The fix is a second winding — the auxiliary or start winding — placed at ninety electrical degrees to the main winding and fed through a capacitor. The capacitor shifts the auxiliary current by roughly 80–90 degrees relative to the main current, and the two fields combine into a rotating field that gives the motor starting torque. Once the motor is up to speed, the job of the capacitor is mostly done, though many motors keep a smaller run capacitor in circuit permanently to improve running power factor and torque.

Main winding versus auxiliary winding

Before you wire anything, identify the two windings with a multimeter on the resistance range. The main (running) winding is wound with thicker wire and has the lower resistance — typically a few ohms on a small motor. The auxiliary winding uses thinner wire with more turns, so its resistance is noticeably higher, often two to four times the main winding. Write the values on a tag and keep it with the motor; guessing wrong here is the most common cause of a motor that runs backwards or burns out its start winding.

On a motor with four leads out of the terminal box, two belong to the main winding and two to the auxiliary. On a six-lead motor the windings are split so the direction can be reversed by swapping the auxiliary leads. Always confirm with the resistance test rather than trusting wire colours, which vary between manufacturers.

Run capacitor and start capacitor

There are two different capacitor jobs, and they must not be mixed up. The run capacitor stays in series with the auxiliary winding all the time the motor runs. It is usually an oil-filled or film type rated for continuous AC duty, typically 20–60 µF on small motors. The start capacitor is a larger electrolytic type, typically 100–300 µF, and it is only in circuit for the first second or two of starting. It is switched out by a centrifugal switch on the shaft (or by a starting relay) once the motor reaches about 75% of full speed. A start capacitor left in circuit will overheat and fail within minutes, sometimes violently, so the switch must work.

Worked example: 1.5 kW pump motor

For a 1.5 kW single-phase pump motor at 230 V / 50 Hz, the rule of thumb of about 70 µF per kW gives a run capacitor of roughly 105 µF. The start capacitor should be about 2.5–3 times the run value, so 250–300 µF. Both capacitors must be rated for at least 400–450 V AC — the voltage across a running capacitor is higher than the supply voltage because of the resonance between the capacitor and the auxiliary winding inductance. A 250 V capacitor in this position will not last.

These are starting points, not exact values. The correct run capacitor is the one that makes the main and auxiliary winding currents roughly equal under load — measure both with a clamp meter and adjust if they differ by more than about 20%.

Wiring steps

  1. Identify the windings with the resistance test and mark the leads.
  2. Connect the run capacitor permanently in series with the auxiliary winding.
  3. Connect the start capacitor (if fitted) in parallel with the run capacitor, through the centrifugal switch or starting relay.
  4. Wire the main winding directly across the supply, through the overload protection.
  5. Check the centrifugal switch moves freely and clicks as the shaft is spun by hand.
  6. Run the motor on no load first, listen for the switch dropping out, and measure both winding currents.

Reversing the direction

To reverse a capacitor-start motor, swap the two ends of the auxiliary winding only — the main winding stays exactly as it is. Swapping the supply leads does nothing, because the motor is not polarity-sensitive. If the terminal box has the auxiliary leads on separate terminals, the swap is a two-minute job; if the windings are joined inside the motor, the end shield has to come off.

Fault finding

  • Motor hums but will not start: open start capacitor, stuck centrifugal switch, or open auxiliary winding.
  • Motor starts but trips the overload: centrifugal switch not dropping out, or the start capacitor left in circuit.
  • Motor runs hot on light load: run capacitor too small or too large, or the wrong value fitted.
  • Capacitor bulged or leaking: voltage rating too low, or a start capacitor that stayed in circuit.

Testing a capacitor before you fit it

Never fit a capacitor on trust. First discharge it through a resistor — a charged capacitor can give a nasty shock even when it has been out of the motor for hours. Then check it with a multimeter that has a capacitance range: the reading should be within about ±10% of the marked value. A run capacitor that reads well below its marked value has dried out and will leave the motor weak and humming; one that reads near zero is open and useless. Also check for bulging, leaking oil or a cracked case — any of those means the capacitor is finished, whatever the meter says. If your meter has no capacitance range, a rough check is to measure the resistance across the terminals: it should rise steadily as the meter charges the capacitor, then settle at a very high value. A steady low resistance means the capacitor is shorted.

Why capacitors fail

Heat is the usual killer. A run capacitor sits in the motor circuit for the whole time the motor runs, and every 10 °C of extra temperature roughly halves its life. Motors in hot plant rooms, motors that start and stop often, and motors fed from a poor supply all wear capacitors faster. Overvoltage is the second cause: a capacitor rated 400 V AC on a supply that regularly peaks above that will fail early, which is why the table above specifies a minimum rating with margin. The third cause is the centrifugal switch sticking closed, which leaves the start capacitor in circuit after the motor is up to speed — it overheats within seconds and usually bursts. If you keep replacing start capacitors on the same motor, check the switch before fitting another one.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use a bigger capacitor than the motor needs?

Not for the run capacitor. A larger run capacitor pushes more current through the auxiliary winding, which overheats it and wastes power. A start capacitor can be a little larger than the minimum, but an oversized one gives a harsher start and stresses the switch. Use the values in the table as a starting point and fine-tune by measuring the winding currents.

Why does my motor hum but not start?

Usually the start capacitor has failed open, or the centrifugal switch is stuck open so the auxiliary winding never gets power. Check the capacitor with a meter and check that the switch clicks as you spin the shaft by hand. If both are good, check the auxiliary winding resistance — an open auxiliary winding gives exactly the same symptom.

How do I know the run capacitor is the right size?

Run the motor under its normal load and measure the main and auxiliary winding currents with a clamp meter. When the capacitor is correct, the two currents are roughly equal. If the auxiliary current is much higher, the capacitor is too big; if it is much lower, the capacitor is too small or has lost capacitance.

Safety note: Capacitors hold a charge after the power is off. Always discharge a capacitor with an insulated resistor or a proper discharge tool before touching its terminals — never short it with a screwdriver, which can weld, spray metal, or damage the capacitor. Work with the supply isolated and locked out.
